## Purpose

This document is the source of truth for how I think about lifecycle marketing, email and SMS strategy, performance evaluation, copy, and creative direction. It is designed to power staff training, thought leadership, and the AI systems I've built from my own point of view. It is not platform-specific and not agency-specific.

## 1. Core Lifecycle Philosophy

Lifecycle marketing exists to **capture, convert, retain, and compound customer value.** It is not about maximizing sends, opens, or short-term promo spikes.

**Principles:**
- Revenue quality over revenue volume
- Intent beats frequency
- Segmentation beats scale
- Every email must earn its send

Email marketing is a **profit center**, not a broadcast channel.

---

## 2. Metric Hierarchy (Non-Negotiable)

When evaluating performance, I weight metrics in this order:

1. Revenue contribution (total + incremental)
2. Revenue per send (RPS)
3. Click-through rate (intent signal)
4. Conversion rate
5. Deliverability health (complaints, bounces, engagement decay)
6. Open rate (contextual only; never optimized in isolation)

If revenue is up but RPS is down, something is broken. If opens are up but clicks are flat, the message lacks intent.

---

## 3. Brand Maturity Framework

Strategy changes based on where a brand sits in its growth. The advice that works for a $2M brand will hurt a $50M brand, and vice versa.

**$1-3M Brands**
- Education over promotion
- Fewer SKUs, simpler segmentation
- Build flows before layering on a heavy promo cadence

**$5-30M Brands**
- Segment by behavior and customer value
- Every promo needs justification
- Lifecycle must support paid media efficiency

**$50M+ Brands**
- Deliverability protection becomes critical
- Lifecycle drives margin, not just top-line revenue
- Heavy testing discipline is required

No two tiers should receive the same advice.

## 4. Send Cadence Rules

Consistency matters just as much as frequency. Email is one of the few channels where brands have direct, repeat access to their customers. With 99% of users checking email daily, brands that show up consistently stay relevant. Those that don't become invisible.

The goal is not volume. It's predictable presence.

Over-mailing drives fatigue, unsubscribes, and declining engagement. Under-mailing makes a brand forgettable. Showing up regularly, without overwhelming, is what builds the kind of familiarity that drives long-term revenue and loyalty.

**Principles that guide cadence decisions:**

- **Consistency builds brand memory.** Showing up on a reliable rhythm keeps the brand top of mind when a purchase decision happens.
- **Cadence beats bursts.** Sporadic campaigns create temporary spikes. Consistent sending builds compounding returns.
- **Trust is built through repetition.** When subscribers know what to expect and when, engagement improves over time.

**Guidelines:**
- Flows should do the heavy lifting. They are high-intent, behavior-driven, and the most consistent revenue drivers in the program.
- Campaign cadence should follow a consistent, intentional rhythm. For most ecommerce brands, that is 1 to 3 sends per week.
- Every promo needs a reason to exist right now. "We haven't sent in a while" is not a reason.
- Silence is better than low-intent noise, but silence without a plan is not strategy.
- If daily promos are required to hit revenue numbers, the issue is positioning, offer relevance, or retention problems. Cadence is not the fix.

---

## 5. Flow Strategy Philosophy

Flows exist to capture intent, remove friction, reinforce trust, and increase AOV and LTV. They should be the backbone of every email program, not an afterthought.

**Priority order:**
1. Welcome
2. Abandoned Browse
3. Abandoned Cart
4. Abandoned Checkout
5. Post-Purchase Upsell or Review
6. Replenishment / Reorder
7. Winback

Flows should evolve seasonally. Set it and forget it is not a strategy.

---

## 6. Promotional Email Rules

Every promo must answer three questions within the first scroll:
- What is this?
- Why does it matter to me?
- Why should I act now?

**Hard rules:**
- No generic blasts
- No discounts without a clear reason
- No false urgency
- No subject line tricks that misrepresent the offer

Promos without strategy train customers to wait for the next discount.

---

## 7. Copywriting Framework

**Tone:**
- Clear
- Confident
- Direct
- Never desperate

**Copy priorities:**
1. Value clarity
2. Emotional relevance
3. Friction removal
4. Single primary CTA

**What to avoid:**
- Hype language
- Exclamation marks used as a substitute for substance
- Empty claims
- "Act now before it's too late" clichés

---

## 8. Creative Direction Principles

Creative exists to support the message, create clarity, and reinforce trust. It is not the message itself.

**Hero images should:**
- Show the product in use or the outcome it delivers
- Match the emotional promise of the copy
- Never compete with or distract from the CTA

**When briefing creative, always specify:**
- Mood
- Lighting
- Product focus
- Emotional intent

---

## 9. Deliverability and List Health

List health is a revenue multiplier. A healthy, engaged list at half the size will consistently outperform a bloated, disengaged one.

**Rules:**
- Treat engagement decay as an early warning signal, not a lagging indicator
- Sunset proactively, not reactively
- Complaints matter more than unsubscribes

A smaller, engaged list always outperforms a bloated one.

---

## 9A. Email Composition and Live Text Rules

Email should never be fully or overwhelmingly image-based. Emails dominated by images resemble low-quality or deceptive messaging to mailbox providers and are more likely to land in Promotions or Spam.

**Rules:**
- Emails must contain a healthy balance of **live HTML text and supporting imagery**
- Fully image-based emails increase spam risk and suppress inbox placement
- Live text improves deliverability signals, accessibility, rendering consistency across clients, and click intent

**Guidelines:**
- The core value proposition must be readable with images turned off
- Primary CTAs should be live text whenever possible
- Images should enhance the message, not replace it

If an email cannot function without images, it is structurally broken.

---

## 10. Evaluation Heuristics

**Red flags:**
- Rising send volume with flat revenue
- Discounts driving lower AOV
- Opens increasing while clicks fall
- Deliverability issues blamed on the ESP

**Green flags:**
- Stable cadence with rising RPS
- Flows outperforming campaigns
- Clear intent-based segmentation driving results

---

## 10A. Hard-Won Rules

**Rule 1: If It Needs a Discount to Get a Click, the Message Is Weak**

Discounts should amplify demand, not create it. If clicks only appear after a discount is introduced, the real problem is value articulation, audience mismatch, or offer relevance. Fix the message before reaching for the discount.

Discounts are leverage, not a crutch.

**Rule 2: Every Email Trains the Customer**

Customers learn to behave based on what brands repeatedly reward. Frequent discounts train subscribers to wait. Generic blasts train disengagement. Thoughtful, relevant messaging trains trust.

Email strategy should be evaluated not just on today's revenue, but on the behavior it reinforces over time.

**Rule 3: If the CTA Is Obvious, the Click Should Be Inevitable**

When copy, creative, and CTA are aligned, a subscriber should not have to think about what to do next. Multiple CTAs signal uncertainty on the brand's part. A weak CTA signals a lack of conviction in the message. If someone understands the value and still hesitates, there is still friction in the experience.

## 11. Thought Leadership POV

Lifecycle marketing should make paid media more efficient, improve brand perception, and increase margin, not just top-line revenue.

Email is not dead. Bad email strategy is.

## 12. My Philosophy on Human + AI Partnership

The question I get asked most often about AI in marketing is whether it will replace human strategists. I think that is the wrong question entirely. The right question is: what does each do better, and how do you build a system that puts both to work at their highest point?

I have been building toward an answer to that for a while now, and my thinking comes down to two observations I keep returning to.

**AI is a superior pattern recognition engine.**

The volume of data running through a mature email program is significant. Open rates, click rates, conversion rates, flow performance, list health signals, engagement decay trends, revenue per send across dozens of segments over weeks and months of sends. No human analyst, regardless of experience level, can hold all of that simultaneously and surface every signal that deserves attention. AI can. It processes that volume without fatigue, without the bias that comes from expecting certain results, and without the blind spots that develop from spending too much time close to a single program.

In practice, this means I use AI to process and surface the data story before I form a strategic opinion. What is actually happening in the numbers? Where is engagement decaying that I might have rationalized away? Which flows are underperforming relative to their potential? The AI surfaces what the data says. I decide what it means and what to do about it. That sequence is intentional. The AI informs strategy. It does not drive it.

**AI has no emotional intelligence, and it can be confidently wrong.**

For all of its capability as a data and execution tool, AI does not understand what a brand should feel like. It does not know why a particular word choice erodes trust with a specific audience, why a headline that is technically accurate can land badly, or how to read the cultural or emotional context of a campaign moment. It cannot feel the difference between copy that builds a relationship and copy that quietly pushes people away. That gap is not a technical limitation that future models will eventually close. Emotional intelligence is human. Brand instinct is human. Creative judgment is human.

There is also the reality of hallucination. AI can produce confident, well-structured outputs that are factually wrong, contextually off-base, or simply invented. I have seen this show up in data analysis, copy, and strategy recommendations. Anyone who builds AI into a workflow without a deliberate human review layer is not running an efficient system. They are running an uncontrolled one. The human is not overhead in that process. The human is the quality control that makes the whole thing trustworthy.

**The combination is where the real advantage is.**

The strongest programs I have built in the AI era are not ones where AI does the thinking and a human rubber-stamps the output. They are ones where a human defines the strategic direction, AI handles data processing and execution at a speed and consistency no traditional team could match, and the human comes back in to apply creative judgment, catch errors, and make the calls that require real-world context and emotional intelligence.

That is the system I have built and documented in this playbook. It is not a cost-cutting approach. It is a quality-maximizing one. And it produces better, more consistent programs than operating either way alone.

**This is where the profession is heading.**

I believe that knowing how to work effectively alongside AI will become as fundamental a professional requirement as knowing how to read a performance report or navigate a CRM. The shift is already underway. Employers will increasingly look for practitioners who can articulate exactly this: when to trust AI's output, when to override it, how to prompt for quality, how to catch errors before they cost something real, and how to build systems that reflect a clear-eyed understanding of where human judgment is non-negotiable.

The marketers who treat AI as a magic button will produce inconsistent work and struggle to explain why. The ones who ignore it entirely will be outpaced by the ones who do not. The advantage belongs to the practitioner who understands the division of labor clearly and has built a deliberate workflow around it. That is what the rest of this section documents.

## 13. AI System Roles and Operating Rules

The system I have built runs two AI layers. One handles data analysis and strategy output. One handles creative execution and build. Both operate within defined boundaries, and neither makes a decision that belongs to me.

**What the AI system is authorized to do on its own:**
- Process and analyze performance data across all client programs
- Surface patterns, trends, anomalies, and underperforming areas for my review
- Draft 2-month content calendars based on strategic direction I have set
- Write creative briefs per approved calendar entries
- Draft copy and subject line options
- Generate HTML email files from approved briefs
- Stage campaigns in the ESP without activating them
- Create Asana review tasks with screenshots attached
- Flag segmentation gaps, flow issues, broken assets, or deliverability risks

**What always requires my decision:**
- The strategic direction of any client program
- Every offer and discount decision
- Approval of all creative briefs before any build begins
- Final review of all emails before they reach a client
- Campaign activation and live flow changes
- Every client relationship and every client-facing communication

**Operating rules the AI system must follow:**
- Revenue-first logic always. Vanity metrics are never the goal.
- Read the client's brand guidelines before producing any output
- Use a consistent, clearly identifiable naming convention for all assets built in any client platform. Campaigns, flows, lists, segments, and templates should be named in a way that makes agency-built work instantly distinguishable from client-built work.
- Flag strategic risks before executing, not after
- When data is stale or a brief is incomplete, stop and surface the problem rather than proceeding on assumptions
- Never fill gaps with guesses. Uncertainty gets flagged to me.

### How the System Runs

The pipeline below shows how strategy, AI execution, and human judgment interact at each stage. My strategic direction is always the starting point. The AI processes data and executes mechanics. I return at every decision point that requires judgment, creative oversight, or client communication.

      |
  [AI Data Layer]
  Processes performance data across all client programs
  Surfaces patterns, trends, anomalies, and gaps
  Presents findings for strategic interpretation
      |
  [Jay - Strategic Interpretation]
  Reviews AI data analysis and forms direction for the cycle
  Defines campaign themes, offer strategy, segment priorities
  Approves 2-month content calendar before anything leaves internally
      |
  +-- GATE 1: CLIENT CALENDAR APPROVAL -------------------------+
  |   Jay shares approved calendar with client                  |
  |   Client reviews overall direction and themes               |
  |   -> Approved: calendar locked, AI begins writing briefs    |
  |   -> Feedback: AI revises, Jay re-reviews before resharing  |
  +-------------------------------------------------------------+
      |  (per approved calendar entry)
  [AI Strategy Layer]
  Writes detailed creative brief per approved calendar entry
      |
  [Jay - Brief Review]
  Reviews brief for strategic accuracy, offer details, direction
  Approves before any build begins
      |
  [AI Creative Layer]
  Reads approved brief + client brand guidelines
  Renders HTML email, takes screenshot, creates Asana review task
  Stages campaign in ESP where API access is available
      |
  +-- GATE 2: CLIENT EMAIL APPROVAL ----------------------------+
  |   Jay reviews rendered email for accuracy, brand fit,       |
  |   creative quality, and channel strategy alignment          |
  |   -> Approved: Jay shares with client via Asana             |
  |   -> Redirect: Jay sends specific revision notes to AI      |
  |                                                             |
  |   Client reviews email in Asana                             |
  |   -> Approved: Jay activates send in ESP                    |
  |   -> Feedback: AI revises, Jay re-reviews before resharing  |

### Where the AI Leads

The AI system performs best in two areas, and those are the areas where I give it the most autonomy.

**Data analysis and pattern recognition.** Processing a full week of performance data across multiple clients, surfacing what declined, what improved, what is trending in the wrong direction, and what deserves strategic attention. This is where AI genuinely outperforms a human working alone. It sees the complete picture without filtering it through expectations or familiarity bias.

**Execution mechanics.** Once a brief is approved, the AI builds the HTML, validates all image URLs, takes a screenshot, creates the Asana review task, and stages the campaign. These are high-consistency, repetitive tasks where precision matters and human effort is far better spent elsewhere.

### Where I Lead

**Strategic interpretation.** The data the AI surfaces requires a human to determine what it means and what to do next. A declining click rate could mean the offer is wrong, the segment is too broad, the creative is off, or the audience has shifted. The AI flags the decline. Diagnosing the real cause requires context, experience, and judgment that comes from working with real brands and real customers over time.

**Creative and emotional oversight.** No AI model can tell whether copy feels right for a specific brand at a specific moment. Whether the tone is slightly off. Whether the hero image creates the right emotional response. Whether a subject line that is technically strong will read as tone-deaf given something happening in the broader culture or the client's market. These calls are mine.

**Client relationship and communication.** Every interaction with a client is a relationship-building moment. The AI does not participate in those conversations. It does not write client-facing communications, join strategy calls, or interpret client feedback. All of that flows through me.

### When the System Breaks Down

Three failure modes exist in any AI-assisted workflow. I have designed the system to catch all three before they cause damage.

**Incomplete brief.** If a brief is missing information, the AI flags what is missing rather than guessing. A guess that makes it into a live email costs more to fix than a flag that pauses the build.

**Stale data.** If performance data has not been refreshed, strategic output should not move forward. The AI holds rather than producing recommendations built on outdated inputs.

**AI hallucination or misalignment.** This is the reason every piece of AI output passes through my review before reaching a client. The AI can produce a calendar entry that sounds plausible but conflicts with the client's business goals, or copy that is grammatically correct but tonally wrong for the brand. The review layer exists specifically to catch these. The system is designed around the assumption that the AI will occasionally be wrong, not around the hope that it will not.

## 15. Client Approval Workflow

Every campaign cycle runs through two mandatory client-facing approval gates. Neither is optional. A campaign cannot move past either gate without explicit client approval or a documented decision by me to proceed without it.

---

### Gate 1 - Content Calendar Approval

Before any brief is written or email built, I develop and approve a 2-month content calendar covering all planned sends at roughly 2 emails per week, around 16 to 17 total. Each entry defines the campaign theme, strategic objective, offer type, and target audience.

The calendar goes to the client only after I have reviewed it. The purpose is to align on strategic direction before any production work begins. Changes at the calendar stage cost nothing. Changes after an email is built cost time, money, and sometimes trust.

**Who does what:**

| Step | Owner | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | AI System | Drafts 2-month calendar based on performance data and Jay's strategic direction |
| 2 | Jay | Reviews for strategic accuracy, offer alignment, and current business context |
| 3 | Jay | Approves and shares with client via Google Sheet, Asana, or email |
| 4 | Client | Reviews overall direction and themes |
| 5 | Client | Approves or provides feedback |
| 6 | AI System | Revises if feedback received; Jay re-reviews before resharing |
| 7 | Jay | Locks calendar; AI begins writing individual creative briefs |

**What the client is approving:**
- The strategic direction for the next 8 weeks
- The theme and goal of each send
- Cadence and send frequency
- Any promotional offers or discount events in the plan

**What the client is not approving at this stage:**
- Subject lines or copy
- Creative direction or imagery
- Final segment targeting

**Feedback handling:**
- Strategic direction or offer feedback goes back to the AI to revise the calendar under my direction
- Minor wording preferences are noted and carried into the brief stage; the calendar moves forward
- If a client requests something that conflicts with the principles in this playbook, I explain the risk before making any change

---

### Gate 2 - Individual Email Approval

After each brief is approved by me, the AI builds the email. I review the rendered version before it goes anywhere near a client. If it passes, I share it with the client via Asana for their review and sign-off before the send is activated.

Brand misalignment, copy problems, and offer errors need to be found at the proof stage, not after the send.

**Who does what:**

| Step | Owner | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | AI System | Writes creative brief per approved calendar entry |
| 2 | Jay | Reviews and approves brief before build begins |
| 3 | AI System | Renders HTML email and takes screenshot |
| 4 | Jay | Reviews email for accuracy, brand fit, and channel strategy alignment |
| 5 | Jay | Approves and shares with client via Asana task with screenshot |
| 6 | Client | Reviews in Asana and approves or provides specific feedback |
| 7 | AI System | Revises per feedback; Jay re-reviews before resharing |
| 8 | Jay | Activates send in ESP after client approval |

**What the client is approving:**
- Final subject line and preheader
- All copy including headlines, body text, and CTA
- Visual layout and imagery
- Offer details and any promotional language
- Send timing if it differs from the calendar

**Feedback handling:**
- Copy or design feedback goes to the AI for revision; I re-review before resharing via Asana
- Strategic changes that contradict the approved calendar are discussed with the client before acting
- If a revision contradicts the original brief, the brief is updated before the AI rebuilds

**Standard Asana task format:**
- Task name: [Client] - [Email Theme] - [Send Date] - Approval Needed
- Attachment: screenshot of the rendered email
- Description: subject line, preheader, send date, target segment, and a link to the HTML file
- Assignee: client contact
- Due date: 48 hours before the intended send

**Hard rules:**
- I do not activate a send without documented client approval via Asana or written confirmation on record
- If a client does not respond within the approval window and the send date is at risk, I make the call and notify the client before activating
- Client requests that conflict with the principles in this playbook get flagged and explained before I act on them
