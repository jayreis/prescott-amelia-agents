## The Problem With How Most Brands Handle Email Today

**In-house teams are stretched thin and strategically siloed.**

Building a high-performing lifecycle program requires deep expertise across strategy, segmentation, copywriting, creative direction, deliverability, platform management, and data analysis. Most in-house teams have one or two people covering all of it. The result is a program that is reactive rather than strategic, focused on getting sends out the door rather than building something that compounds customer value over time.

In-house teams also lack external perspective. They see their own data, their own creative, and their own assumptions, often for years. The patterns that should trigger a program overhaul go unnoticed because they have become the new normal. A seasoned outside strategist brings fresh eyes, cross-brand pattern recognition, and benchmarks that only come from working across many brands and categories at the same time.

**Most agencies have not figured out AI yet, and the ones that have are using it wrong.**

Agencies that have adopted AI are largely using it to replace headcount. They are asking AI to write copy, generate subject lines, and produce creative without meaningful human oversight. The output looks like email. It rarely performs like a strategically crafted one.

The agencies that have not yet adopted AI are falling behind on data processing, speed, and consistency. Neither camp has landed in the right place.

My position is straightforward: AI is not a replacement for human expertise. It is a precision instrument that amplifies it, when applied responsibly, in the right places, by people who understand its real limitations. I call this approach Responsible AI, and it is the foundation of how I operate — documented in full in `prescott/sops/responsible_ai_policy.md`.

## Why Strategy-First Beats Send-First Every Time

Most email programs are managed send-to-send. A campaign goes out, performance is glanced at, and the next one gets scheduled. There is no system underneath it. The program does not get smarter. It just keeps running.

The approach I take starts with a different question: what is this program trying to build, and is every send actually contributing to that?

**Revenue quality beats revenue volume.** A program generating $50K per month from eight highly targeted sends to your most engaged segments is worth more than one generating $55K from twenty generic blasts, because the first is building customer behavior that compounds and the second is training subscribers to wait for the next discount and tune out everything else.

**Your highest-value customers deserve a different experience than everyone else.** Identifying which customers have the highest lifetime value, which are at risk of churning, and which are likely to buy again in the next 30 days, then treating each group accordingly, is what separates lifecycle programs that drive compounding LTV from ones that just drive opens. Most agencies segment by recency and frequency. I segment by customer value trajectory.

**Flows are the backbone, not the afterthought.** Automated behavior-triggered flows, welcome sequences, abandoned cart, post-purchase, replenishment, winback, consistently outperform campaign sends on a revenue-per-email basis because they reach the subscriber at a moment of genuine intent. Most brands have flows that exist but do not perform. Auditing, rebuilding, and evolving those flows as customer behavior changes is where the highest-leverage work happens.

**Every email trains the customer.** Brands that discount frequently train subscribers to wait. Brands that send generic content train disengagement. Brands that show up with relevant, well-timed messaging build trust over time. The right question is not "what should we send this week?" It is "what behavior are we reinforcing, and is that the behavior we actually want?"

**Segmentation beats scale.** Sending to a smaller, more precisely targeted segment consistently outperforms blasting the full list. Deliverability improves. Revenue per send improves. Long-term list health improves. The instinct to maximize reach is understandable. The data consistently argues against it.

## Strategy Tailored to Where You Actually Are

One of the most common agency failures is applying the same playbook to every client regardless of where they are in their growth. The strategy that protects margin at a scaled brand will stall growth at an emerging one. The promotional cadence that works for a mature list will damage deliverability for a brand still building its audience.

I apply a structured maturity framework to every client engagement (see `prescott/playbooks/Lifecycle Revenue Playbook v3.md`), and the advice changes meaningfully at each stage.

**Early-stage brands:** Education before promotion. Build the flow infrastructure before layering campaign volume on top of it. Simpler segmentation, tighter targeting, and a focus on establishing the foundation that everything else will build from.

**Growth-stage brands:** Behavioral segmentation and customer value tiers. Every promotional send requires strategic justification. The lifecycle program must actively support paid media efficiency, retaining and upgrading the customers that paid acquisition brought in so the overall unit economics improve over time.

**Scaled and enterprise brands:** Deliverability protection becomes mission-critical. Lifecycle drives margin, not just top-line revenue. Testing discipline, proactive list health management, and a focus on moving customers up value tiers rather than simply acquiring more of them.

I bring experience across all three stages. On a personal level, I scaled a DTC brand from zero to $1M in revenue within twelve months. At the enterprise level, I've worked with brands that have operated globally for over a century. That range, from founding-stage resourcefulness to enterprise-grade discipline, is what I bring to every client engagement.
